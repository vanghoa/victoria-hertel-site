## Sacrificial Protection, 2023

<br />
![Alt text](/sacrificialProtection/1.jpg)
<br />
*Sacrificial Protection* is a tech-based collaboration between artist Chok Si Xuan, curator Seet Yun Teng, and myself. The works in the following section stem from the fusion of our individual practices into a collective endeavor.
<br />
Developed through the lens of tropical technologies, we constructed frameworks for human and non-human interaction. Viewing climate not merely as a meteorological backdrop but as an active elemental participant, each framework embraced the influential weathering over the two-month exhibition period. Categorised into *[Light]*, *[Rain]*, and *[Heat]*, each space harnessed an ambient characteristic of tropical zones—equatorial daylight hours, heavy rainfall, humidity levels, and high temperatures with minimal fluctuations. By selecting an off-the-grid Singaporean construction site as our venue, we were challenged to power all works solely through solar energy. This enmeshment with solar cycles fostered natural intervals of activity (when power was generated) and rest (when solar exposure was low), prompting a reevaluation of the need for constant activation, the pace of technological acceleration based on intensive resource extraction, and the perceived divide between technology and the environment.
<br />
In keeping with urban construction aesthetics, most of the structures were made from staple building materials such as corrugated metal sheets, galvanised steel mesh, and repurposed steel rebars. This approach allowed the installations to integrate seamlessly into the locational context while maintaining features that distinguished them as temporary, site-specific interactive structures.

**Key words: weathering, flash rust, oscillation**  
<br />
*Sacrificial Protection [Light]* is a tech-based installation that translates alternating light levels into vibrational decay. Light sensors on site monitored changing brightness thresholds, activating vibration motors attached to steel mesh pods in response. With equatorial daylight and nighttime hours nearly equal, half of the pods responded to bright light while the other half reacted to low light exposure. The bare steel pods continuously formed flash rust, which flaked off due to the motor vibrations. This slow disintegration mimicked urban decay and the skeletal remnants of construction projects. As the motors corroded from tropical weathering, their mechanical whirring resembled the sound of buzzing insects, merging urbanism with tropical fauna.
<br />
Sourcing small-scale solar panels with battery extensions enabled the connection and powering of all the 5V electronic components used in the works. Although the production, material implications, and end-of-life management of solar panels have their own dark side, compared to the burning of finite fossil fuels, solar energy generation presents a cleaner alternative—reducing water use, minimising air pollution, and offering extensive renewability.
<br />
<br />
**Overview [ Galvanised Cocoa ]**  
*Sacrificial Protection [Light]* featured cocoa-seed-like structures crafted from galvanised steel wire mesh, a material commonly used in urban fencing. Their botanical aesthetic emerged from a desire to blend the tropical flora, found in lush micro-pockets on site, with its urban backdrop of skeletal construction remnants.

<br />
**Detail [ Flash Rust ]**  
Small photodetectors monitored the changing light levels on site. In response, vibration motors attached to the steel activated, causing the pod to cyclically flake off its accumulated flash rust in a process of buildup and erosion.

**Close-Up [ High Acidity ]**  
Each pod was marinated in vinegar over the span of a couple of days to strip it of its sacrificial zinc-coating. The high acidity left the remaining steel frame bare, enabling a quicker rusting process through the unprotected exposure to the tropical environment.

**Key words: meteorological transitions, rain harvesting, electroacoustics**  
<br />
*Sacrificial Protection [Rain]* is a tech-based installation that circulates water between electro-mechanical and organic networks. After harvesting rainwater in water-resistant nylon catchalls, *[Rain]* funneled the collected water through a glass separatory system, before releasing it onto an atomiser plate. The atomiser then evaporated the droplets back into their surroundings via electroacoustics, creating a subtle soundscape of faintly hissing vapour.
<br />
To conserve resources post-deinstallation we recycled all the steel used on-site through a metal reclaiming company. Although metal reprocessing reduces the need for mining and consumes less energy than producing from raw materials, it also poses environmental impacts through emissions and coating contaminations. By sourcing our large gridded support structures second-hand, we aimed to further reduce our overall metal carbon footprint.
<br />
<br />
**Overview [ Rain Harvest ]**  
*Sacrificial Protection [Rain]* harvested heavy tropical rainfall. Our structures caught the falling water in nylon cloth, channeling it into glass funnels for temporary storage before gradually releasing it back into the air via water atomisers. Returning what was borrowed.

**Detail [ Mirror Glass ]**  
Our funneling system was sourced from a local laboratory glass blowing centre. Due to minor defects, the funnels were unsuitable for lab purposes, but their reflective properties functioned to mirror viewers back into their surroundings upon closer inspection of the stored water.

A coin-sized water atomiser dispersed the collected rain droplets back into the environment as cool water vapour. The metallic plate functioned as an electroacoustic transducer, converting drops into micro droplets using high-frequency mechanical oscillation. This process produced a paradoxically soft hissing sound that was only perceptible through deep listening.

**Key words: archipelagos, ambient temperature, ecoacoustics**  
<br />
*Sacrificial Protection [Heat]* is a tech-based installation that translates thermal changes into ecoacoustic soundscapes. Equipped with microcontroller-linked temperature sensors, the five corrugated steel structures of *[Heat]* responded to specifically coded Celsius intervals within the tropical temperature range of 24°C to 40°C. Motors nestled between the sheet structures began to spin and strike the undulated metal surfaces, creating a clanging sound—a hybrid between urban construction noise and rainfall sequences. While weather conditions primarily activated the installation, the motor-sensors could also be triggered by body heat when held onto, blending human and non-human activation.
<br />
In an effort to minimise our environmental impact, all electronics from the *[Heat]* section were disassembled, cleaned, and stored for future use in our tech-based practices. Striving to reduce e-waste—one of the fastest-growing global waste streams—we repurposed functioning components and recycled those that were heavily damaged.
<br />
<br />
**Overview [ Echoes ]**  
*Sacrificial Protection [Heat]* was an archipelago of corrugated steel sheets, arranged to leisurely stroll through. The concrete platform into which the sheets were anchored was flanked by large buildings on each side. This configuration reflected any sound generated by our structures back into the space, adding a layer of discovery in determining which mechanisms were active.
<br />
![Alt text](/sacrificialProtection/heat1.jpg)
<br />
<br />
**Detail [ Mono-Ha ]**  
Though we had anchored the panels, we arrived one morning to find them crumpled by storm currents. To mitigate the site‘s architectural wind tunnel effect, we repurposed rebar cut-offs from *[Rain]* together with rock debris as Mono-Ha-esque reinforcement angles.
<br />
![Alt text](/sacrificialProtection/heat2.jpg)
<br />
<br />
**Close-Up [ Urban Soundscapes ]**  
Each steel island emitted a metallic soundscape reminiscent of construction clanging and raindrops on sheet roofing. This sound was produced by motors nestled between the corrugated sheets, which rapidly struck or brushed against the wavy surface in a circular trajectory.
<br />
![Alt text](/sacrificialProtection/heat3.jpg)
<br />
<br />
**Close-Up [ 2°C ]**  
Heat sensors attached to the steel scanned temperature fluctuations, triggering in 2°C intervals (26-28°C to turn on, 28-30°C to turn off, etc.). The changes were communicated to the structures‘ motors via the use of microcontrollers, allowing each one to respond to its unique temperature state. A Real-Time Clock module enabled the controllers to track time, switching the circuits on and off, delegating control back to the circuit itself instead of requiring manual adjustments.
